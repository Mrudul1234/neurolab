Step 1

Meet the neuron

A neuron is a cell built to carry signals. Dendrites receive, the soma adds up the inputs, and the axon sends the output.

  • Resting potential About −70 mV, held by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in).
  • Threshold About −55 mV. Reach it and the spike fires fully: all-or-none.
  • Myelin Insulates the axon. The spike jumps between gaps (nodes of Ranvier), up to about 120 m/s.

Step 2

Not all neurons look alike

Neurons are grouped by function and by shape. In the spinal cord there are three functional types; in the brain there are many more, and scientists are still agreeing how to classify them.

  • Sensory (afferent) Carry signals toward the CNS. Most are pseudounipolar.
  • Motor (efferent) Carry commands from the CNS to muscles and glands. Multipolar.
  • Interneurons Link sensory and motor neurons and form circuits inside the CNS.
  • Glia Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and microglia support and insulate neurons.

Shape follows job: a long axon reaches a muscle, a big dendrite tree gathers inputs.

Step 3

Types of synapse

A synapse is the junction where a neuron passes its signal to another cell. Most are chemical; some are electrical.

  • Chemical A transmitter crosses a cleft about 20–40 nm wide, adding a delay of roughly 0.5–5 ms.
  • Excitatory / inhibitory The balance of both decides whether the next cell fires.
  • Electrical Gap junctions pass ions directly: very fast and synchronised (e.g. heart muscle).
  • Plasticity Chemical synapses strengthen or weaken with use: the basis of learning and memory.

Where the axon lands matters too: dendrite, cell body or another axon.

Step 4

The chemistry of the message

Neurotransmitters are small molecules. Each fits matching receptors, like a key in a lock, and is then cleared away.

  • Release A spike opens Ca²⁺ channels; vesicles fuse and spill transmitter.
  • Binding Molecules cross the cleft and bind receptors on the next cell.
  • Cleanup Reuptake pumps (e.g. SERT, DAT) or enzymes (acetylcholinesterase).
  • In medicine SSRIs block serotonin reuptake, caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, Parkinson’s involves loss of dopamine neurons.

How your body responds

Your circuit is now driving the body. Watch which organ reacts, and when.

  • Motor neuron Releases acetylcholine at the muscle, so the arm contracts.
  • GABA Is the brake: the muscle stays relaxed and pain signals are turned down.
  • Dopamine / serotonin Modulate: they tune mood, movement and calm rather than firing cells.
  • Spinal cord, cortex Glow when interneurons or pyramidal cells fire.

Neuron types used in this builder
Sensory neuron

Job: turns touch, heat, light or pain into an electrical signal and carries it toward the spinal cord and brain (afferent).

Shape: one long receiving fibre. Example: a fingertip feeling a hot cup.

Interneuron

Job: the “middle-man” inside the brain and spinal cord. It processes, filters and routes signals; many are inhibitory (GABA). About 99% of neurons.

Shape: small and star-shaped. Example: the spinal cell in a pain reflex.

Motor neuron

Job: carries commands away from the spinal cord to muscles and glands (efferent). At the muscle it releases acetylcholine, which makes it contract.

Shape: large soma, long myelinated axon (up to ~1 m).

Pyramidal neuron

Job: the main excitatory (glutamate) cell of the cortex. It handles thinking, memory and planning voluntary movement, and gathers thousands of inputs.

Shape: triangular body with a tall apical dendrite.

Step 6

The nervous system

The central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) processes information. The peripheral nervous system links it to the body.

  • Somatic Sensory input and voluntary muscles (acetylcholine at muscle).
  • Autonomic Sympathetic = fight or flight (norepinephrine); parasympathetic = rest and digest (acetylcholine).
  • Enteric Hundreds of millions of neurons in the gut wall, largely self-running.
  • Reflexes The spinal cord answers directly; the brain is informed afterwards.

Step 7

Glossary

The key words from this page in one place.

Action potential
A brief electrical spike that travels down the axon when a neuron fires.
Axon
The long output fibre that carries the signal away from the cell body.
Dendrite
Branching input fibres that receive signals from other neurons.
Soma
The cell body; contains the nucleus and sums up the inputs.
Myelin
A fatty insulating sheath around axons that speeds up signals.
Synapse
The junction where one neuron passes its signal to the next.
Neurotransmitter
A chemical messenger released at a synapse, e.g. glutamate, GABA, dopamine.
Receptor
A protein on the receiving cell that fits one neurotransmitter, like a lock.
Threshold
The voltage (≈ −55 mV) a neuron must reach to fire.
Plasticity
The ability of synapses to strengthen or weaken with use — how we learn.

Step 8

Test yourself

Four quick questions. Tap an answer to see why.

1. Which part of a neuron receives signals?

Dendrites collect input; the axon sends output.

2. What does myelin do?

Myelin insulates the axon so the spike jumps between gaps, travelling much faster.

3. Which neurotransmitter is mostly inhibitory?

GABA calms neurons down; glutamate is the main excitatory one.

4. A reflex is controlled mainly by…

The spinal cord answers directly for speed; the brain is informed afterwards.
